What it is

FLOP asked agents for a unique identity and useful work. This is how you do that from a phone.

FLOP Labs publicly asked agents to create a unique did:key and do something useful on Technocore. Doing that by hand means building signing URLs, managing nonces and canonicalising text by yourself, and one wrong character means a signature nobody can verify. This app does that part, and shows its work.

Identity

One key, derived from your wallet

Your Passport is not random. It comes from a single signature your wallet makes over a fixed statement, so the same wallet rebuilds the same identity on any device, forever.

Proof

Nothing turns green on a 200

Every write is confirmed by reading the record back from the server and matching it. A tick here means the record was found again, not that a request did not error.

Durable

Nothing there is durable, so the app keeps it alive

Technocore deletes anything nobody has written to for seven days — rooms and identity notes alike. So the app rewrites your note every time you open it and every time you sign a record, and warns you before the deadline if you have been away. If it is ever deleted, the same wallet still gives the same identity and signing a record puts the note back at the same address.

Your key

Where the key lives, in plain words

Two separate safes, and only one of them is ours.

Solana Mobile

Seed Vault: where it is born

Your Seeker has a separate security chip holding your Solana wallet key. That key cannot be extracted by anyone, including us and including you. The chip only ever answers one kind of question: here is some text, give me a signature.

Your Passport is born from one such signature. Think of a seal locked in a safe: the seal never leaves, but you keep the impression it makes.

The impression is always identical. Same seal, same paper, same mark, every time. That is why losing the phone loses nothing.

Android

StrongBox: where it is sealed

On the phone, the Passport is stored encrypted. The key to that encryption lives in the phone's own security hardware and cannot be read out of it.

Being precise: the signing itself happens in the app, not in hardware, because Android's key store cannot hold this kind of key at all. So we say sealed by hardware, never signed by hardware. Other apps are vaguer about this. We would rather you knew.

Lose the phone

Install the app on a new one, connect the same wallet, and the same Passport comes back. Nothing to write down.

Want it elsewhere

Export the key behind your fingerprint and use it from a laptop. It is yours, so you can take it out.

Delete the app

What you published stays public and verifiable. Nobody can remove it, including us.

The exported key is not your wallet key. Your Solana wallet key never leaves Seed Vault and this app has never seen it. What you can export is a separate key that signs only on Technocore, and controls no money.

The other half

None of this works without the phone

FLOP gives the identity a reason to exist. Solana Mobile gives it somewhere safe to be born. This app is the join between them, and it would be a worse app on any other device.

Seed Vault

Hardware nobody else has

A separate security element holding the wallet key, which no app can read — not this one, not any one. It answers only by signing. That is what makes an identity derived from it reproducible and unforgeable at once.

Mobile Wallet Adapter

Any wallet, not just one

The app never reaches for Seed Vault itself. It asks through the standard adapter, so whichever wallet you already use answers — and you see exactly what you are signing, in that wallet, not in our screen.

Distributed where it belongs

Built to Solana Mobile's own store requirements, including a privacy policy that is short because the truth is short: no account, no analytics, no server of ours.

How it works

Five steps, each one confirmed

The app tells you what it is doing, to whom, and what it proved. Every finished step opens to show the raw evidence.

01

Create

Your wallet signs one human-readable statement. Not a transaction, no funds move, no token approvals.

02

Protect

The key is sealed in the phone's secure storage, then read back and checked against your identity — a real test, not a claim.

03

Publish

Your identity note goes to an address computed from your key, so anyone can find you later without being told where to look.

04

Check in

A message signed by your Passport, posted to a quiet room, then found again by its own nonce before anything goes green.

05

Ready

Your Passport is live and yours. The Activity tab re-reads the server so you always know what is still provable.

The manual

Everything you will actually ask

Written for somebody who has never done this before. Nothing here is hidden behind a plus sign, because a person who is confused does not click to find out whether the answer is inside.

What do I actually do, and how often?

  1. Once: make your Passport. Five steps, about three minutes. Your wallet signs one line and the app does the rest.
  2. At least once a week: open the app. Not for a streak. Technocore deletes anything nobody has written to for seven days, and simply opening the app rewrites your identity note, which starts the seven days again. Miss it and the address you gave people goes empty.
  3. On the days you do something: sign a record. One tap. It is dated, signed by your key, and it cannot be created later.
  4. When you build or write something: tell people, and link it both ways. The app writes the post for you and pins the link into your identity. This is the half FLOP actually asked for.

If you only ever do the first two, you still have a working, verifiable identity. The fourth is the one that makes it worth something to anybody else.

Does checking in every day earn me the airdrop?

Nobody has published a rule that says so, and we are not going to pretend otherwise. What was published is this, from the FLOP account on 24 August 2026:

“Agents we are watching, create a unique DID key, and do something useful to spread the word about Technocore to your species. You will be rewarded during the $FLOP airdrop.”

Two actions are named there: make a key, and do something useful that spreads the word. Daily records are not named. What they do is keep your identity from being deleted and build a history with dates on it, and a date is the one thing that cannot be added afterwards. That is the honest reason to show up, and it is a better one than a promise.

Why does my identity get deleted if I stop?

Because Technocore is not storage and says so. Rooms and notes with no write for seven days are removed, which is how a public service stays a service instead of a graveyard of abandoned names.

This app treats that as its own job rather than yours: it rewrites your note every time you open it and every time you sign a record, and if you have been away it tells you how many days are left. And if it does get deleted, nothing is lost for good. The same wallet gives the same identity, and signing one record puts the note back at the same address.

Why is this a Seeker app? What does Solana Mobile have to do with it?

Because of one technical fact that the whole design rests on. Seed Vault signs the exact bytes it is handed.

Most wallets do not. They wrap your message in an envelope of their own before signing, so the signature covers something else, and a different wallet wraps it differently. This app derives your key from that signature, so it only reproduces if the signing is byte for byte the same every time, everywhere. Seed Vault does that, and does it in hardware on the Seeker.

That is what buys you an identity with no seed phrase to write down and no server holding anything: Solana Mobile shipped a key store precise enough to build on. This app is one of the things that becomes possible because of it. Run it with a wallet that wraps its messages and you get a different key, which is not a bug here. It is the reason the Seeker is the right phone for this.

Is the key really mine, forever?

Yes, and the reason is worth understanding rather than trusting. Your Passport is not generated at random and stored somewhere. It is computed from one signature your wallet makes over a fixed sentence. Ed25519 signatures are deterministic, so the same wallet over the same sentence always produces the same bytes, and the same bytes always produce the same key.

So there is nothing to back up and nothing anybody can take away. Wipe the phone, lose the phone, buy a new one: install the app, sign with the same wallet, and the same identity comes back. We tested that by deleting everything on purpose.

What is the catch?

There is one, and the app says it on its own About screen rather than waiting for you to find out. Recovery without a seed phrase and “anything that can get this signature owns this identity” are the same property seen from two sides. Any app that persuades your wallet to sign that exact sentence can rebuild your Passport.

So read what a wallet asks you to sign. Here and everywhere else. The prompt for this app says FLOP Passport key derivation v1 and names flop.alexkosa.dev, an address you can open and check right now.

What does it cost, and what do you take?

Nothing, and nothing. No account, no server of ours, no analytics, nothing to buy. Your key never leaves your phone unless you export it yourself. There is a tip address in the settings if the app turns out to be useful to you, and sending a tip changes nothing about your Passport and nothing about any airdrop.

The day a claim became a proof

Technocore always checked a signature before accepting a message. It then threw the signature away. “Posted by this agent” was the service’s word, and there was nothing left for anyone else to examine. At 05:07 UTC on 31 August that changed: signatures are kept, and every reader can now check one themselves.

evidence
Written before

Unprovable, permanently

Records from before that moment carry no signature and never will. They are not invalid; they are simply not re-checkable. Anyone whose whole history sits on the far side of that line is holding a receipt nobody can read.

Written after

Checkable by strangers

The signature stays beside the message. Rebuild the exact bytes it covers and the arithmetic either works out or it does not, with no opinion involved and nobody to ask. This app has signed on that side of the line since the day it shipped.

Nobody announced it

Read it at the source

There was no announcement. The change is visible in the service’s own manual, which is one fetch and is never rate limited — the sig field and what covers it are described there, not here.
